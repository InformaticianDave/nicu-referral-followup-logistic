# Methods and statistical analysis

Written for collaborators without a statistics background. Applies to
both outcomes and all three variable groups.

## 1. Methods

This pipeline uses bootstrapped multivariable logistic regression to
identify factors associated with two discharge outcomes in a NICU
population: whether an infant was **referred** to outpatient
developmental/coordination services, and, among those referred,
whether they **attended follow-up** or were **lost to follow-up
(LTFU)**. Logistic regression is the appropriate method whenever the
outcome is binary (yes/no), which both of these are.

Predictors are grouped into three domains and modeled separately
(clinical_continuous, clinical_categorical, sociodemographic) so each
result table stays a manageable size for a clinical audience rather
than one unwieldy table mixing a dozen unrelated variable types.

## 2. Statistical analysis

Classical logistic regression reports a p-value and confidence
interval from the Wald test, which assumes the sampling distribution
of each coefficient is approximately normal -- a good approximation
with large, balanced samples, but shaky with:

- a modest overall sample size,
- unequal group sizes (e.g. far fewer LTFU events than successful
  follow-ups), and
- categorical predictors with sparse cells (e.g. only a handful of
  infants in a given race/ethnicity x insurance combination).

To avoid relying on that assumption, this pipeline **bootstraps**:
it re-fits the logistic regression on `n_boot` (default 200) random
resamples of the training data (same size, drawn with replacement),
and builds the confidence interval and p-value directly from the
resulting distribution of odds ratios, rather than from a formula that
assumes normality.

- **Effect / Odds Ratio** is `exp(coefficient)` from the model fit on
  the full (non-resampled) training data.
- **95% CI Lower/Upper** are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the
  bootstrap odds-ratio distribution (the "percentile method").
- **P-value (Bootstrap)** is twice the smaller of the proportion of
  bootstrap odds ratios below 1 vs. at or above 1 -- i.e. how often
  the resampled estimate disagrees in direction with "no effect",
  capped at 1.0.

Bootstrapping is preferable here because it does not assume
normality or a large sample; it produces confidence intervals and
p-values that reflect the actual shape of this dataset's distribution,
at the cost of extra computation (one model fit per resample).

### Reading a result row

Each categorical predictor needs one level held out as the
**reference/baseline category** (listed in
`config/variable_groups.yml`) so the model is identifiable --
otherwise the dummy columns would be perfectly collinear. Every other
level's **Effect (odds ratio)** is interpreted *relative to that
reference*: an OR of 2.0 for `Insurance_type_Private` means the odds
of the outcome are twice as high for infants with private insurance
compared to the reference category (public), holding everything else
in the model constant. An OR below 1.0 means lower odds relative to
the reference; an OR of 1.0 means no difference.

Continuous predictors (e.g. `GA`, `delivery_bw`) don't need a
reference -- their Effect is the odds ratio per one-unit increase.

### Performance metrics (held-out test split)

| Metric | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| ROC AUC | Probability the model ranks a random positive case above a random negative one | Summarizes overall discrimination independent of a chosen probability cutoff; 0.5 = chance, 1.0 = perfect |
| Accuracy | Fraction of test infants classified correctly | Easy to explain, but can be misleading with class imbalance |
| Precision | Of infants predicted positive, the fraction that truly were | Matters when false positives are costly (e.g. unnecessary referral workload) |
| Recall | Of infants who were truly positive, the fraction the model caught | Matters when missing a true case is costly (e.g. missing an infant who needs follow-up) |

## 4. Discussion / limitations

- Mode/median imputation is simple and may bias estimates if
  missingness isn't random (carried over from the phase-1 R
  exploration; see `docs/phase1_notes.md`). A future iteration could
  add multiple imputation as a sensitivity check.
- Several clinical predictors are correlated (e.g. GA, birth weight,
  and length of stay). Bootstrapping doesn't remove multicollinearity,
  it only avoids assuming normality in the resulting estimates --
  check variance inflation if two predictors in the same group are
  highly correlated.
- A very high ROC AUC (e.g. > 0.95) on a small test split can also
  signal data leakage (a predictor that is only observed *after* the
  outcome occurs) rather than genuinely strong separation -- always
  sanity-check which predictors are truly knowable at the point the
  outcome is being predicted.
